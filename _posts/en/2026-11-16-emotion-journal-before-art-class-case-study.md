Picking one feeling was never the hard part. What came next was. One emotion per artwork, vocabulary that stopped at "happy," "excited," "bored" — and joy painted yellow, every time.

Two elementary school teachers redesigned the lesson around exactly that. They left the worksheet, the materials and the prompts untouched, and added one thing in front of the class: **four days of keeping an AI emotion journal**. This qualitative case study was presented on 14 November 2026 at the joint autumn conference of the Korean Society for Educational Technology and the Korean Association for Educational Information and Media, held at Konkuk University.

## The lesson

"The Emotion Flower That Bloomed in Me" (Introducing Myself Through Art), a curriculum-linked SEL art lesson, run as two periods of 80 minutes.

Students explore feelings and the situations behind them, find the name and colour of an emotion on a Mood Meter, sketch petals with line, shape and colour, render them on tissue paper with water-based markers and spray them so the colours bleed, bind the petals into a flower, and then talk through it: the name, the feeling, why that colour and that shape, and what they do to take care of themselves.

The two teachers co-developed the material and piloted it in the first semester of 2026.

## The problem the teachers saw

Students had no trouble choosing a feeling and starting to draw. But the Mood Meter's finer vocabulary went almost unused, and the emotions recalled on the spot during class connected only loosely to real situations.

The teachers concluded that what was missing was not a longer word list. What was missing was **raw material — feelings and situations worth expressing**.

## What they changed

They placed a Seamspace AI emotion journal in front of Activity 1 as a warm-up.

- The prompt: "Write down something memorable from today along with how it felt." Free writing.
- Twice a day, before dismissal and before bed. Morning entries were excluded over concerns about bias.
- At least four days before the lesson, on students' own phones.

In class, instead of recalling a feeling on the spot, students **re-read their own entries and the app's analysis** and chose what to express from there.

The design is a qualitative case study following a plan–act–observe–reflect cycle. Sixteen fourth graders in one homeroom class (led by their teacher, linked to a Korean language unit) and five fifth graders in an after-school art club took part. The same students' first artworks (before the app) and second artworks (after journaling), along with interviews, were compared. There was no control group and no statistical testing. Guardian consent, child assent and pseudonymisation were in place, and app data was limited to the number of entries and whatever students described themselves.

## What the teachers observed

**Starting and staying with it.** "Can I look at my emotion journal while I work?" — then straight into their own entries. Unlike the restless opening of the earlier lesson, students settled into the work almost immediately, the teachers observed.

**A wider range of emotional awareness.** Three club students had been monotone in the first round; in the second they reached for "looking forward to it," "hopeful," "happier than ever." Students in the homeroom class newly noticed feelings that mixed enthusiasm, pride, embarrassment and gratitude. The teachers read the shift as appearing **in awareness before vocabulary**, noting that "it did not go as far as expanding vocabulary, but experience with a variety of emotion words accumulated."

**Form changed, colour did not.** Lines and shapes did change — overlapping, curves, two images in one piece. But **colour stayed conventional.** The newly discovered feelings (resentful, cosy) did not make it into the artwork either. Written records, the teachers judged, did not translate directly into visual expression.

**Taking the AI's reading — and doubting it.** When the app picked out "calm" and "cosy" alongside an entry about a happy day at a grandparent's house, many students accepted it: "So that's how I feel about my grandmother's house." But one student was upset when the word "giving up" surfaced in an entry about skipping a cram school class they did not want to attend. The researchers framed this carefully: the app did not judge the emotion, it **suggested emotion words drawn from the writing**, and questioning a mismatch was itself a form of re-recognition.

**What students said helped.** The stage they found most useful was planning — and they pointed to writing and re-reading before colours or graphs. "The app leaves comments, so it was more fun than writing a diary alone and I ended up writing more honestly."

## The teachers' reflections

One student always chose "joy." Learning that the same student still chose "joy" after discovering "anger" changed how the teacher understood that artwork. Watching and tracking students' emotion records, the teachers said, helped their teaching.

The limits were just as clear. An emotion journal is writing, and writing does not directly help a child give visible form to an invisible feeling. Turning text into image **requires the teacher's questions and scaffolding**. As one teacher put it, this is the point where "both edtech and person-to-person teaching are needed."

## Suggestions you can use in class

1. Place the journal as **scaffolding for the planning stage** — a twice-daily routine in the week before the lesson. The goal is not producing vocabulary but widening the range of feelings available to express.
2. Bridge text to image **with teacher questions**: "Does that feeling have to be that colour?" "What colour was that moment?" Compare the Mood Meter's colours with the colours of a memory.
3. Treat the AI's reading as a **suggestion, not an answer**. Add a short activity where students say whether they agree with the emotion word, and why.
4. Invite students to put a feeling they met for the first time in their journal into the flower.

## Limits the study states for itself

- A small sample over a short period (16 fourth graders, 5 fifth graders, roughly four days of entries).
- Because the same lesson ran twice, the app's contribution cannot be separated from the effect of repetition.
- Instructor interviews and blind ratings are to be added at the paper stage.
- On the ethical sensitivity of AI analysing children's private writing, the scope of access and the crisis-response procedure were agreed in advance.

## What we took from it

Teachers in the classroom designed and documented a way of using Seamspace as "a warm-up right before the lesson." We are noting two things: that the most useful place in the journal → analysis → lesson sequence was **the planning stage**, and that the process of a student agreeing or disagreeing with an AI-suggested emotion word is itself part of the learning.
