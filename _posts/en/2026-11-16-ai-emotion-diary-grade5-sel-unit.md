A fifth-grade classroom in Gyeonggi Province. All fourteen lessons opened the same way: for the first fifteen minutes students wrote down how they felt that day and why, and an AI replied with empathy. Then they made posters, wrote songs, acted out scenes, drew webtoons and played together.

The homeroom teacher designed and ran the unit and took part as the researcher. This mixed-methods study was presented on 14 November 2026 at the joint autumn conference of the Korean Society for Educational Technology and the Korean Association for Educational Information and Media, held at Konkuk University.

## Why the study was done

In a school-community survey, the thing students worried about most in school life was friendships (45.5%). The topic teachers most wanted to cover in the school's discretionary curriculum hours was "physical and mental health" (41.7%).

Existing research on AI emotion journals has largely centred on pre- and post-test scores. **What students actually wrote, and how they experienced it,** has been less visible. That is why this study looked at the survey (perceived change) alongside the journals and reflections (experience).

## How it ran

- **Participants:** 21 students in one fifth-grade class at an elementary school in Gyeonggi Province, first semester of 2026
- **Programme:** a 14-session "mental wellbeing" unit within "A Healthy Body and Mind," once or twice a week over about ten weeks, developed using the ADDIE model
- **Lesson structure:** 15-minute AI emotion journal at the start (write your mood and the reason; AI responds with empathy) → cooperative group activity → closing (sharing, resolutions, self-assessment)

The unit was built as six modules: ① exploring Seamspace and "my weather today" ② emotion posters (Canva) ③ empathising with a friend through an AI-generated song (SUNO) ④ role-play and webtoons with a co-teacher (Tooning) ⑤ small-group cooperative games ⑥ looking back and reflecting. The design moves emotions outward from text to image to sound, taught by two adults working together.

Seamspace was used as the journal tool. As the researchers describe it, a student writes about their day, the AI identifies emotions and responds with empathy, and the teacher follows the class's emotional trend through a dashboard.

Guardians and students were informed about the use of research data and gave consent, and all analysed and quoted material was anonymised.

## What was collected and how it was analysed

A four-item survey of perceived social-emotional competence (5-point scale, mid-March and early July, n=19) was analysed with paired-samples t-tests and Wilcoxon tests (Cronbach's α = .76). The 216 AI emotion journal entries (21 students, 12 rounds between 21 April and 22 June) and 18 programme reflections were coded against a codebook and categorised. Two coders worked independently; agreement rose from κ = .44 in the first round to κ = .90 in the second.

One point is worth stating plainly. **Seamspace's own emotion indices were not used in the change analysis.** The researchers judged them to be strongly influenced by the journal topic of the day.

## Findings

**Perceived competence (survey).** The four-item mean went from 3.33 to 3.91 (t(18) = 3.41, p = .003, Cohen's d = 0.78, Wilcoxon Z = 2.81, p = .005). Of 19 students, 12 rose, 5 stayed the same and 2 fell. The largest gains were on "helping a friend in difficulty" (+0.79) and "managing my feelings" (+0.63); the smallest was "cooperating in group work" (+0.36). **That said, this was a single class of 21 students with no control group, so the change cannot be attributed to the programme.**

**What appeared in the journals (216 entries).** At least one social-emotional competence appeared in 184 entries (85%).

- Emotional awareness and regulation — naming an enjoyable event and why, 58; distinguishing mixed feelings, 53
- Building positive relationships — growing closer to a friend, 41; repairing a relationship after conflict, 23; doing things together and helping each other, 10
- Self-management — reviewing and assessing oneself, 29; making resolutions and plans, 23; getting things done, 12
- Responsibility and empathy — knowing and keeping rules, 33; courtesy and community behaviour, 19; considering another's position, 12; caring about someone's pain, 5

Some of what students wrote (anonymised as in the original):

> "A kid called ○○ teases me a lot … I'm a bit angry, but it was still happy and fun, so it was good." (18 June)

> "I want to get angry, but I hold it in because I'm afraid my friends will dislike me." (21 April)

> "The friends who had left me out came to me and said, '○○, we're sorry we played without you.'" (22 May)

> "In April I went through school life with the goal of not running in the hallway." (22 April)

**What appeared less.** Accounts of expressing and then calming an emotion numbered 13, and accounts of doing things together and helping each other numbered 10 — both low. Most described holding it in, or the feeling easing with time.

**Reflections on the AI (18).** Enjoyable activity, 11; an AI that helps me know myself, 8; an AI that empathises, 3; moments of boredom, 3. "At first I thought, AI has no feelings and it's a robot, so maybe it won't understand? But it actually empathised really well…" and "It was a little boring, but I liked being able to tell how I was feeling."

## How the researchers read it

**Emotional regulation happened less in what was written than in the act of writing.** The survey showed a rise in managing feelings, yet the journals contained few accounts of self-calming. Students' reflections were "writing without hiding my feelings made it easier" and "it felt like my chest opened up." This touches prior work suggesting that writing and naming an emotion softens it. The researchers propose journal prompts that ask about regulation strategies, alongside lessons on those strategies.

**In relationships, growing closer showed up; cooperating did not.** Many entries described becoming friends through play, while group cooperation was lowest both in the survey gain and in the journals. Hence the suggestion to place a reflective journal prompt immediately after group activities.

**The AI was an empathetic listener.** Students experienced it as something that would hear them out at any time. Lecture-style outside sessions were boring, but activities where they wrote and took part themselves carried meaning. The researchers suggest connecting what a student tells the AI to conversations and activities with peers and teachers.

**The AI is the teacher's assistant.** When an AI reply stops at generic encouragement, the teacher has to fill the gap; and the teacher uses the dashboard and the journals to read the class's emotional trend and spot students who need support. The conclusion is that the AI supports noticing and intervening rather than replacing the teacher.

## Suggestions you can use in class

- **Design the prompts.** Different prompts surfaced different competences, so space out prompts that ask about regulation strategies, cooperative experiences and other people's feelings.
- **Order the lesson.** Put the journal right after cooperative learning or play, so students reflect on relationship skills while the experience is fresh.
- **Fill the gap as a teacher.** Check the dashboard and the journals, pick up the difficulties the AI did not address, and step in directly.
- **Further research.** A comparison group, measurement of all five sub-factors, repeating the same prompt, and analysis of how the AI's replies express empathy.

## Limits the study states for itself

- A single class of 21 students with no control group, so the change cannot be attributed to the programme.
- A four-item survey measures social-emotional competence only in part.
- There is no systematic analysis of the student–AI interaction (the AI's replies).
- The survey sample is n = 19; the 216 journal entries are 12 rounds from 21 students.

## What we took from it

A school designed and documented a case where the journal lives inside regular lessons as a "first fifteen minutes" routine. We will take two points into our product and guidance material: that different journal prompts surfaced different competences, and that when an AI reply stops at generic encouragement, the teacher needs to fill the gap.
